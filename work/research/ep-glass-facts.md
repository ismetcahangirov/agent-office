## 0. The hook and the one-sentence answer

**Contradiction (from the episode brief):** sand is opaque and gritty, yet glass is clear and smooth.

**Why sand is opaque (simple physics, RECORD):**
- Sand and glass are both mostly silicon dioxide. Sand "has obvious grain boundaries, so it is not transparent" (Susan Murphree Thomas, Georgia Tech). A material looks transparent "if the distance between boundaries is smaller than the shortest wavelength of visible light". Sand "is so filled with impurities that light simply scatters outward incoherently" (Morton Tavel, Vassar College).
  https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/what-determines-whether-a/ *(opened)*
- **Say it simply:** "Every grain of sand has edges. Billions of edges bounce light around, so you can't see through a pile. Melt the grains into one smooth piece, and the edges disappear. Light goes straight through."

**Why soda matters (RECORD):**
- Pilkington (NSG Group), "Chemistry of glass": silica sand "has a high melting temperature in the region of 1700 degrees C"; "the high melting point and viscosity of silica is reduced by adding sodium oxide (a flux)".
  https://www.pilkington.com/en/global/knowledge-base/glass-technology/chemistry-of-glass *(opened)*
- CMOG glossary: a flux is a substance that lowers the melting temperature; soda and potash are fluxes.
  https://allaboutglass.cmog.org/definition/flux *(search summary)*
- Egypt, about 1250 BC (Qantir): the first melt was at about 900–950 °C. Colouring and ingot-making was at about 1000–1100 °C (Rehren & Pusch 2005, *Science* 308: 1756).
  https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/7099/ *(search summary)*
- **Say it simply:** "Sand alone needs about 1,700 degrees. That's hotter than any ancient fire could get. Add soda, and the mix starts to melt at around 1,000. The soda is the shortcut."
- **Lime** (calcium) makes glass stable. Without it, soda glass slowly dissolves in water. *(verify: I found no first-hand source this session. Drop it or source it before use.)*

**Proposed one-sentence answer:** Nobody "invented" glass in one moment. Potters and metalworkers kept finding glassy crusts in their fires. Over about 2,000 years people learned the trick (sand plus soda plus a very hot fire), then learned to blow it, then to make it perfectly clear and flat.

**Twist:** the most famous origin story (Pliny's sailors on a beach) is one Pliny himself only gave as "the story goes" (*fama est*). And the first glass people ever used was not made by people at all. Nature made it: volcanoes and a meteorite.

## 2. Stage / reason candidates (13 checked, best 6–7 marked ★)

Each item: story (person, place, date) → one striking number → status → source → script note.

### ★ A. Glass before humans: nature made it first · RECORD
- **Story 1 (Tutankhamun's scarab):** Howard Carter found a pectoral in Tutankhamun's tomb in 1922. He listed its yellow-green scarab as chalcedony (a stone). In 1998 the Italian mineralogist Vincenzo de Michele showed it was Libyan Desert Glass. *(search summary: Atlas Obscura / Archaeology magazine; the 1998 paper itself was not opened, verify)*
  https://archaeology.org/issues/september-october-2019/digs-discoveries/trenches-egypt-tut-desert-glass
- Libyan Desert Glass formed about **29 million years ago**, covers "thousands of square kilometres" of western Egypt, is "nearly pure silica", canary yellow, and appears "in a scarab ornament within King Tutankhamun's chest pectoral". Cavosie: only meteorite impacts "create shock waves that form high-pressure minerals", and that is what they found.
  https://www.curtin.edu.au/news/media-release/curtin-planetary-scientist-unravels-mystery-of-egyptian-desert-glass/ *(opened)*
- **Story 2 (obsidian mirrors):** Çatalhöyük, Turkey, Neolithic. James Mellaart (1960s) identified at least 6 obsidian objects as mirrors. Naomi Hamilton later listed 11 mirrors or possible mirrors. The surfaces are "highly polished and reflect a sharp image" and are "slightly convex".
  https://archive.archaeology.org/online/news/mirrors.html *(opened)*
- Obsidian is volcanic glass. People shaped it and polished it, but they could not melt it or make more.
- **Striking number:** 29 million years (Tut's scarab glass) vs about 3,300 years since Tutankhamun.
- **Script:** "The first glass a human ever held was made by a volcano. The most famous piece was made by a rock from space." Fast and visual, so a good **chapter 1**.
- **Reject:** fulgurites (lightning glass). Real, but I found no source for human use. Drop.

### ★ B. The happy accident in the kiln: glaze → faience → beads · RECORD (beads), DISPUTED (exact origin)
- Faience: a quartz body with an alkaline glaze; "first appeared at the end of the fifth millennium B.C."; it may imitate turquoise and other blue stones; the glaze was "an early forerunner" of glass (Met). *(search summary)*
- Getty (Antonaras): glass was "possibly" "a very fortunate accident during experimentation with glazed pottery or faience", "probably" in the 3rd millennium BC, "in all likelihood in Mesopotamia". *(opened)*
  https://www.getty.edu/publications/ancient-glass/intro/
- CMOG: glassmaking "likely" grew "over a long period of time from experiments with a mixture of silica-sand (ground quartz pebbles) and an alkali binder fused on the surface". It started as a coating on clay pots. Then people found thicker pieces could stand alone. "at least 40 centuries ago." *(opened)*
  https://libanswers.cmog.org/faq/143701
- Rehren & Freestone 2015: the earliest glass objects (mid-3rd millennium BC) were beads, perhaps accidental by-products of metalworking (slag) or faience-making. *(search summary)*
- **Striking number:** "at least 40 centuries" (4,000+ years).
- **Script:** "Nobody set out to invent glass. A potter wanted a shiny blue coat on a bead. Sometimes the coat got thick enough to stand on its own."

### ★ C. The royal recipe: Bronze Age glass factories, letters and a sunken cargo · RECORD
- **Qantir-Piramesses, Egypt, ~1250 BC** (Thilo Rehren, UCL; Edgar Pusch, Pelizaeus Museum Hildesheim): a two-step process. Raw materials were heated "in recycled beer jars", crushed and washed. Then the glass was coloured (mostly red, with copper) in special crucibles and cast into round ingots for other workshops. The glass was made from crushed quartz pebbles, not sand. *(Scientific American, opened; quartz-pebble detail is a search summary of the UCL release)*
  https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/ancient-egyptian-glass-fa/
- **Amarna letter EA 323 (~1350 BC):** Yidya of Ashkelon to Pharaoh: since the king ordered glass, "I herewith send … 30 (pieces) of glass". British Museum BM 29836. *(Wikipedia, opened; Moran 1992 translation, verify)*
- **Uluburun ship (~1320 ± 15 BC, off Kaş, Turkey):** excavated 1984–1994 by INA (George Bass, then Cemal Pulak). It carried glass ingots. Jackson & Nicholson 2010 showed that blue and turquoise ingots are Egyptian glass, probably headed for the Mycenaean world.
  https://nauticalarch.org/projects/uluburun-late-bronze-age-shipwreck-excavation/ *(opened)* ; https://www.aegeussociety.org/en/new_article/the-provenance-of-some-glass-ingots-from-the-uluburun-shipwreck/ *(opened)*
- **Striking numbers:** 30 pieces of glass as a royal order (EA 323); about 175 ingots on one ship *(verify the count in Pulak's publications)*.
- **Script:** "Glass was a royal treasure. Kings wrote letters asking for it, a few pieces at a time." Note: people at the time listed glass alongside precious stones (Assyrian *zagindurû* glass is named after lapis lazuli). *(verify the lapis etymology before using it)*

### ★ D. The secret instruction tablets · RECORD (with a sensitive detail, see §7)
- **Object:** BM K.2520, "Fragmentary cuneiform tablet with glass recipes", Neo-Assyrian, reign of Ashurbanipal, about 650 BC, Library of Ashurbanipal, Kouyunjik (Nineveh). 27.5 × 10.5 cm. *(ISAW/NYU, opened)*
  https://isaw.nyu.edu/exhibitions/ishtar-gate/objects/tablet-glass-recipes
- Bryn Mawr review of K. Schmidt (2019): a group of **five** tablets from Ashurbanipal's library deals with making glass from raw materials. The process needed several rounds of "heating, crushing, and melting". Schmidt sees them as true recipes reflecting 2nd-millennium-BC practice. *(opened)*
  https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/?p=44160
- Thavapalan 2021: the texts describe Bronze Age techniques. They are mostly known from later copies in Ashurbanipal's libraries. She publishes new Middle Assyrian fragments in the Yale Babylonian Collection. *(opened, abstract)*
- **Translated lines** (ORACC "glass" project, P393786, via a secondary blog; **verify at** https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/glass/P393786, which failed with an SSL error this session):
  - "When you lay the foundations of a glass-making kiln, [you search repeatedly for a suitable day] during a favorable month…"
  - "…in order that an outsider or stranger cannot enter; one who is impure cannot cross…"
  - Wood: "thick logs of poplar that are stripped … (these logs are to be) cut during the month of Abu…"
  Source of the excerpt: https://meshandcloth.substack.com/p/passing-down-the-craft-the-oldest *(opened; secondary)*
- **Striking number:** about 3,300 years since the first glass instructions (CMOG FAQ).
- **Script:** "This is the oldest how-to manual for glass. Step one: pick a lucky month. Step two: keep strangers out." Do not mention the kūbu figures or the sacrifice (§7).

### ★ E. Pliny's beach legend, and what the chemistry says · LEGEND (story) + RECORD (chemistry)
- **Pliny, NH 36.191** (AD 77): "fama est" ("the story goes") that a ship of nitrum (natron) merchants landed near the river Belus. They found no stones to hold their cooking pots, so they used lumps of natron from the cargo. When these burned and mixed with beach sand, "tralucentes novi liquores fluxisse rivos" ("transparent streams of a new liquid flowed"); "et hanc fuisse originem vitri" ("and this was the origin of glass").
  Latin: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Pliny_the_Elder/36*.html *(opened)*
- Bostock & Riley (1855): "The story is, that a ship, laden with nitre, being moored upon this spot, the merchants, while preparing their repast upon the sea-shore, finding no stones at hand for supporting their cauldrons, employed for the purpose some lumps of nitre which they had taken from the vessel." The beach strip was "not more than half a mile in extent". Their footnote: "Beckmann discredits this story, because sand, he says, is not so easily brought to a state of fusion." *(opened, peterburk mirror)*
- Pliny 36.190 places the spot in "Phoenice", near Mount Carmel. Strabo (16.2.25) says the Belus sand was carried to Sidon to be melted. So the sand was real and famous.
- CMOG: Pliny's account "is unverified". *(opened)*
- **Why chemists doubt it:** a cooking fire on a beach rarely gets hot enough, for long enough, to fuse sand and natron into glass. Bronze Age furnaces worked at about 900–1100 °C for long periods (Qantir, Rehren & Pusch 2005). Pure silica needs about 1,700 °C (Pilkington). Also, glass already existed about 2,000 years before Pliny (Getty, CMOG).
- **Counterpoint (DISPUTED):** a 1960s industry article reports that a wood fire kept going for two hours in the open reached 1,200 °C, "sufficient to melt" a soda-silica glass. *(J. Soc. Cosmetic Chemists 13(1); https://library.scconline.org/v013n01/17, opened; old, secondary)*
- **Striking number:** about 2,000 years between the first real glass and the story that claims to explain it.
- **Script:** "Pliny himself starts the story with 'the story goes'. Even he wasn't sure." This is the best "myth vs record" beat. Put it **near the middle** as a pattern interrupt.

### ★ F. One breath changes everything: glassblowing makes glass cheap · RECORD (core), DISPUTED (speed and cheapness)
- Getty: invented in the first half of the 1st century BC "somewhere in the Syro-Palestinian area". Blowing made glass "much easier and more economical". By the mid-1st c. AD it spread to Italy, Switzerland and Dalmatia. *(opened)*
- Jerusalem, Jewish Quarter: Nahman Avigad's 1971 dig found workshop waste from about **50–40 BC**: glass tubes with one end closed and blown into a bulb, and small striped bottles. This is still "the earliest reasonably closely datable evidence of glassblowing". *(CMOG online publication, search summary)*
- **Strabo 16.2.25** (about AD 20): in Rome "many discoveries are made both for producing the colours and for facility in manufacture … as, for example, in the case of glass-ware, where one can buy a glass beaker or drinking-cup for a copper". *(opened)*
- **Seneca, Letter 90.31** (about AD 64): "a glass-blower who by his breath moulds the glass into manifold shapes". *(opened)*
- **Before blowing:** core-forming. You wind hot glass around a clay core on a rod, cool it, and scrape the core out. It was slow and only good for small perfume bottles (CMOG glossary; search summary).
- Met (Roman cameo glass essay): blowing in the late 1st c. BC was "one of the most significant technological advances of the ancient world". *(search summary; the Met returned 429)*
- **Ennion** (1st half of 1st c. AD): a Sidon-linked glassmaker who signed his mould-blown cups. Met objects are CC0 (see §6).
- **Scale later:** Bet Eli'ezer (Israel, 8th c. AD) tank furnaces each held at least 8–10 tonnes of glass (Gorin-Rosen 1995, 2000) *(search summary)*.
- **Striking number:** one copper coin for a drinking cup (Strabo).
- **Script:** this is the strongest stage. Put it late-middle (~60–70%).

### G. Roman windows: the first see-through walls · RECORD (with honesty caveats)
- Museo Galileo: "The first visitors to Herculaneum and Pompeii in the course of the 1700s and 1800s, were astonished by the quantity of glass still present in the window frames." Specular stone (thin mica or selenite sheets) was also used. *(opened)*
- Seneca 90.25: "windows which admit the clear light through transparent tiles". Note: the Latin is *specularia*, which can mean panes of stone (lapis specularis), not glass. *(opened)*
- Roman window glass was cast on a table: thick, greenish, not fully clear. *(search summary: VELUX history; https://en.villumwindowcollection.com/history-of-the-window/roman-time, direction)*
- **Script:** good as a 2–3 sentence part of F or of the finale, not a full chapter.

### ★ H. The island of secrets: Venice, Murano and cristallo · RECORD (1291, cristallo), DISPUTED (secrecy laws)
- 982: first Venetian glassmaker named in a document ("Dominic"). *(Museo Correr, opened)*
- 1291: "The Grand Council … decreed the transfer of the furnaces to outside the city's urban core to avoid the risk of fire." *(opened)*
- Secrecy: the glassmakers' guild had rules from about 1271 (the *capitolare*), replaced by the *Mariegola* in 1441. Workers were forbidden to work abroad. Some emigrated anyway, "regardless of the prohibitions and heavy penalties". Some got permission to leave during the summer furnace shutdown (*cavata*, mid-August to January). *(CMOG and Wikipedia, search summary; exact penalties not checked)*
- Cristallo: Angelo Barovier (c. 1405–1460) purified the plant-ash flux. The result rivalled rock crystal ("cristallo"). "By 1455" he was established as its inventor. *(CMOG search summary; Museo del Vetro opened: "completely pure and like rock crystal")*
- Barovier Cup (blue, about 1470) is in the Museo del Vetro. *(opened)*
- **Striking number:** 1291, the year a city moved a whole industry to an island because of fire.
- **Script:** "Venice moved its glassmakers to an island. The official reason was fire. The bonus was that secrets are easier to keep on an island." Keep penalties out (§7).

### I. England's answer: Ravenscroft and lead glass · RECORD (patent), DISPUTED (who "invented" lead crystal)
- 1674 patent: "a perticuler sort of Christaline Glasse resembling Rock Cristall". From 1676–77 a raven's-head seal marked his "improved" glass. The marked pieces are still cloudy. "After 1677, Ravenscroft likely resolved these issues by adding lead oxide, effectively inventing lead glass." *(V&A O5153, opened)*
- **Script:** at most one sentence ("England tried to copy Venice and ended up with something new: heavy, sparkly lead glass"). Optional.

### ★ J. Flat and perfect: Pilkington's float glass · RECORD
- Alastair Pilkington began experimenting in December 1952. In July 1958 a sheet was made entirely by float. Announced 20 January 1959. Cost £28 million ("approximately £150 million in today's money"). There are now "around 500 glass float lines in the world". *(Pilkington heritage, opened)*
- The process: molten glass floats on a bath of molten tin, so it comes out flat on both sides with no grinding or polishing. *(search summary of Pilkington pages)*
- **Striking number:** about 500 float lines make almost all the world's window glass. *(only "around 500 lines" is sourced; "almost all" is my wording, say "most")*
- **Script:** the **finale**. It closes the loop: sand → perfectly flat, perfectly clear window → your phone and your windows.

### K. Natron to plant ash: the soda supply · RECORD (secondary role)
- Until about the 9th c. AD, glass across the Mediterranean used natron (mineral soda from Egypt, mainly Wadi Natrun, about 100 km northwest of Cairo). Then glassmakers switched to soda-rich plant ash. *(search summary of Shortland et al. 2006 and IFAO)*
- Bet She'arim slab: about 9 tonnes of glass that went wrong, probably a 9th-century experiment that mixed old Belus-type sand with the new plant ash. *(search summary of Freestone & Gorin-Rosen 1999 and CMOG blog)*
- **Script:** good **"Imagine you're…"** moment ("You've just melted 9 tonnes of glass. It comes out cloudy. You walk away and leave it there for 1,100 years.") Or use it as the backup quiz.

### L. Tiberius and the "flexible glass" · LEGEND
- Pliny NH 36.195: "ferunt" ("they say") that under Tiberius a glass mix was invented "ut flexile esset" (so that it would bend), and the craftsman's whole workshop was destroyed so that copper, silver and gold would not lose value. Pliny adds: "eaque fama crebrior diu quam certior fuit", "this story was long more widespread than it was certain". *(Latin opened)*
- A darker version is in Petronius (*Satyricon* 51). **Do not use it** (§7).
- Modern echo: Corning's Willow Glass (2012) bends, at about 100 microns thick. *(search summary of Corning release)*
- **Script:** a great **stinger** at the end: "Pliny didn't believe the bending-glass story. In 2012 a glass company made glass you can bend."

### M. Earliest glass "invented in Egypt" or "by the Phoenicians" · REJECT as stated
- The evidence points to Mesopotamia first (3rd millennium BC beads). Egypt began deliberate making under Thutmose III (15th c. BC), first importing from the Near East. The Phoenicians are linked to the Pliny legend and to later Sidon production, not to the invention. (Getty; Met; CMOG)

**Recommended running order (6 chapters, Answer Puzzle words):**
1. **NATURE**: glass before humans (A). Fast and visual.
2. **ACCIDENT**: kiln glaze → beads (B).
3. **RECIPE**: royal glass and the instruction tablets (C + D).
4. **LEGEND**: Pliny's beach, and why soda is the real hero (E). Myth-vs-chemistry at about 50%.
5. **BREATH**: glassblowing makes glass cheap, plus Roman windows (F + G). Strongest stage, at about 70%.
6. **ISLAND → FLAT**: Venice and cristallo, then float glass (H + J). Stinger: flexible glass (L).

## 3. Cold-open candidates (first 15 s, clean for advertisers)

**CO-1 (strongest): "Cairo, 1998. The stone that isn't."**
> Cairo, 1998. You're standing in front of a boy-king's gold jewellery. Gold wings. Blue and red inlays. In the middle, a yellow-green beetle. For 76 years, the label said it was a stone. You look closer. It isn't a stone. It's glass. And it's 29 million years old.
- Then: "Nobody made it. A meteorite did. So how did humans learn to make their own? How did we invent glass?"
- Sources: Curtin 2019 (29 Ma, Tut's pectoral) *(opened)*; Carter's 1922 identification as chalcedony and de Michele 1998 *(search summary, verify)*. "76 years" = 1922 → 1998. "A boy-king" is fine, but don't mention his death or the mummy.
- Second person: "you" here are a visitor, not de Michele. Don't call yourself the discoverer.
- Leads to the title question in about 8 s.

**CO-2: "A beach near Mount Carmel, the story goes."**
> A beach on the eastern Mediterranean, two thousand years ago, the story goes. You're a sailor. Your ship is full of white blocks of soda. There are no rocks for your cooking pot, so you prop it up on the cargo. The fire burns all night. In the morning, something shiny is running through the sand. It's glass. That's the most famous story about how glass was invented. It almost certainly didn't happen.
- Sources: Pliny 36.191 (*fama est*, *opened*); CMOG "unverified" *(opened)*. "Burns all night" and "in the morning" are **not** in Pliny. Pliny only says they lit the fire and transparent streams flowed. **Trim to:** "The fire roars. Then something shiny starts to flow out of the sand."
- Then: "So if not like this, how did humans really invent glass?"

**CO-3: "Rome, around AD 20. One copper coin."**
> Rome, around the year 20. You walk into a shop. Noise from the street. A shelf of cups so clear you can see the wall through them. You hand over one copper coin, and one is yours. Fifteen hundred years earlier, kings had to write letters to get thirty pieces of the same stuff.
- Sources: Strabo 16.2.25 *(opened)*; EA 323, 30 pieces *(opened, Wikipedia/Moran)*. "Fifteen hundred years" ≈ 1350 BC → AD 20 = about 1,370 years. **Say "more than a thousand years earlier"** to stay honest.
- Then: "Same material: melted sand. So how did humans invent glass, and how did it go from treasure to cheap?"

## 5. Where sources disagree

1. **When was glass first made?** Getty: 3rd millennium BC, "possibly", "in all likelihood in Mesopotamia". CMOG: "at least 40 centuries ago", western Asia, "possibly Mesopotamia". Rehren & Freestone: sporadic beads mid-3rd millennium; regular production about 1500 BC. A 1960s source mentions claims as early as 7000 BC (old, ignore). **Script:** "The first glass beads show up around 4,500 years ago, probably in Mesopotamia. Real glass workshops come about a thousand years later."
2. **Mesopotamia or Egypt first?** The traditional view is that Mesopotamia came first and Egypt imported. Rehren & Pusch 2005 showed large-scale Egyptian primary production by about 1250 BC, so Egypt "played a more substantial role than previously understood" (Scientific American). **Script:** "Mesopotamia probably got there first. Egypt soon became a glass factory too."
3. **The Pliny legend.** Pliny himself hedges (*fama est*). Beckmann (via Bostock & Riley) and CMOG doubt it. A 1960s test reportedly reached 1,200 °C with an open wood fire after two hours. **Script:** "Most experts doubt a beach cooking fire could do it. And glass already existed for 2,000 years before Pliny wrote it down."
4. **Where was glassblowing invented?** Getty: "somewhere in the Syro-Palestinian area", first half of the 1st c. BC. The Met: "late first century B.C.". The earliest datable evidence is Jerusalem, about 50–40 BC (CMOG). **Script:** "The oldest blown glass we can date comes from a workshop dump in Jerusalem. The invention itself happened somewhere on that coast."
5. **Did blowing make glass cheap fast?** Strabo says a cup cost one copper coin. Larson 2019 (*J. Roman Archaeology* 32: 7–22) argues adoption needed a whole reorganisation of supply, workshops and training, so it was gradual. Pliny 36.195 also says that under Nero two small cups (*pteroti*) sold for "HS VI". Translations read this as 6,000 sesterces or as 6,000 sestertia (much more). **Don't put a price on it.** Say "luxury glass could still cost a fortune".
   https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-roman-archaeology/article/cheap-fast-good-the-roman-glassblowing-revolution-reconsidered/4118C6A250083B0331819FA8287BD64C *(opened, abstract)*
6. **The Assyrian tablets' date.** CMOG: "circa 3,300 years ago". The surviving Nineveh copies are about 650 BC. Thavapalan and Schmidt: they copy Bronze Age (2nd-millennium) knowledge. **Script:** "The copies we have are about 2,700 years old. The knowledge inside is older."
7. **Uluburun.** INA: "ca. 1320 ± 15 B.C.". Dendrochronology: after 1305 BC. Ingot count "about 175" is only on Wikipedia/secondary. **Script:** "about 3,300 years ago", and "a cargo of glass ingots", with no number unless you verify it.
8. **Venice 1291: fire or secrecy?** Museo Correr: fire risk. Popular sources: also to control the glassmakers. CMOG: secrecy rules (travel bans) came with and after the move. One source says 1292. **Script:** "The official reason was fire. Keeping secrets was a bonus."
9. **Seneca's "transparent tiles".** Museo Galileo reads Seneca as calling glass windows a recent invention. The Latin *specularia* can mean panes of mica or selenite. **Script:** "Some Roman windows had glass. Others used thin slices of a clear mineral."
10. **Ravenscroft.** Patent 1674 is certain. That he "invented lead crystal" is "likely", after 1677 (V&A). **Script:** "His glass kept going cloudy. Adding lead seems to have fixed it."
11. **Çatalhöyük mirror dates.** Archaeology magazine: no exact date. MAA 2016: Çatalhöyük 7400/7100–6200 BC. Popular sites: "8,000 years". **Script:** "around 8,000 years ago" is safe.
12. **Libyan Desert Glass cause.** It was debated for decades (airburst vs impact). The 2019 study found high-pressure evidence for an impact. **Script:** "most likely a meteorite impact".

## 6. Modern-life comparisons and talking objects

**Comparisons (adults):**
1. **Your share of glass:** about 21 kg of new glass per person per year, about the weight of a checked suitcase *(the comparison is my approximation)*. Source: Westbroek et al. 2021.
2. **Dollar-store glass:** Strabo's "drinking-cup for a copper" ≈ "the Roman version of a one-dollar glass", 1,300+ years after kings were ordering glass by the piece (EA 323).
3. **The world's first non-compete clause came with its own island:** Venice moved its glassmakers to Murano (1291) and banned them from working abroad. Today, companies make you sign a form. *(Keep it light. Don't call it a "prison".)*
4. **Your window is a 1959 invention:** before float glass, clear plate glass had to be ground and polished. Now about 500 float lines make it (Pilkington).
5. **Bendable glass:** Pliny dismissed it as a rumour. Corning sells it (Willow Glass, 2012). Use as the stinger. Don't name the brand on screen if you want it neutral.

## 8. Sensitive / avoid list (RULES §7, §12.3) and honesty lines

**Keep out (or keep to one neutral clause, never in the first 30 s):**
1. **Assyrian ritual details:** the tablets mention *kūbu* figures (often translated "embryos" or stillborn demons) and a sheep sacrifice. **Out.** Say: "pick a lucky month, make an offering, keep strangers out."
2. **Tiberius story, Petronius version:** the inventor is beheaded. **Out.** Use only Pliny's version (workshop closed) and his own doubt.
3. **Venetian penalties:** the popular "death penalty / assassins for glassmakers who fled" stories. **Out.** Say "banned from working abroad".
4. **Pompeii / Vesuvius:** don't explain why the windows survived (the eruption and deaths). Say "Roman houses near Naples" or "Herculaneum", with no disaster.
5. **Obsidian:** blades, arrowheads and the mirrors' burial context. **Out.** Mirrors only, "found in houses".
6. **Tutankhamun:** no mummy, death or curse talk. "A young king's jewellery."
7. **Natron:** it was also used in mummification. **Out.**
8. **Lead glass health claims** ("lead crystal is poisonous") and glassworkers' illnesses or burns. **Out** (health claims).
9. **Uluburun:** "a trading ship that sank". No crew deaths (none are known anyway), no "wreck horror".
10. **Modern politics:** Nineveh/Mosul (recent war damage), Jerusalem/Israel/Palestine, Lebanon. Use neutral geography: "Nineveh, in today's northern Iraq"; "the eastern Mediterranean coast". Avoid the word "Palestine" or "Syro-Palestinian" in narration (scholarly term, may read as political). Say "the eastern Mediterranean coast" or "the Levant coast".
11. **Wine and beer:** Qantir's "recycled beer jars" is a fun detail but drink-related. Say "old clay jars".
12. **Weapons / military glass** (optics, bulletproof glass): not needed. **Out.**
13. **Meteorite "catastrophe" language:** keep it at "a space rock hit the desert", no "mass extinction" or "threat to Earth".

**Honesty lines (don't say X → say Y):**
- ✗ "Phoenician sailors invented glass." → ✓ "A Roman writer, Pliny, told a story about sailors. He started it with 'the story goes'."
- ✗ "A campfire can't melt glass, ever." → ✓ "Most experts doubt a beach cooking fire could do it. Ancient glassmakers needed furnaces running hot for hours."
- ✗ "Glass was invented in Egypt." → ✓ "The first glass beads probably come from Mesopotamia. Egypt became a big glassmaker later."
- ✗ "Glass was invented in 2500 BC." → ✓ "The oldest glass beads are about 4,500 years old."
- ✗ "Ancient glass was made from beach sand." → ✓ "Some was. Egypt's glassmakers crushed quartz pebbles instead."
- ✗ "Glassblowing was invented in Jerusalem." → ✓ "The oldest blown glass we can date was found in Jerusalem."
- ✗ "Thanks to blowing, everyone had glass overnight." → ✓ "Within a few decades a simple cup could cost one copper coin in Rome (Strabo). Fancy glass stayed expensive."
- ✗ "Romans had clear glass windows like ours." → ✓ "Roman panes were small, thick and greenish. Some windows used thin slices of a clear mineral instead."
- ✗ "The Assyrian tablet is the first glass recipe, from 650 BC." → ✓ "The copies we have are about 2,700 years old. The recipe inside is older."
- ✗ "Venice moved glassmakers to Murano to protect its secrets." → ✓ "The official reason was fire. The secrecy came with it."
- ✗ "Barovier invented clear glass." → ✓ "Barovier made a glass so clear Venetians named it after rock crystal: *cristallo*." (Romans already made nearly colourless glass with manganese or antimony; RSC Education, https://edu.rsc.org/download?ac=15150, search summary)
- ✗ "Ravenscroft invented crystal in 1674." → ✓ "He patented a new clear glass in 1674. A few years later, adding lead seems to have fixed it."
- ✗ "Pilkington invented float glass in 1952." → ✓ "He started experimenting in 1952. It was announced in 1959."
- ✗ "Romans invented unbreakable glass and the emperor hid it." → ✓ "Pliny reports a rumour, and says himself it was more widespread than certain."
- ✗ "Sand melts at exactly 1,700 °C; soda makes it melt at 1,000." → ✓ "Sand alone needs about 1,700 degrees. With soda, the mix starts to melt at around a thousand."
